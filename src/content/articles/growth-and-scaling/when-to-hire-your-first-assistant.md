Every agent who hits a certain production level asks the same question: do I need help?

The honest answer is yes — and you probably needed it three months before you started asking.

The harder question is: what kind of help, when, and how do you structure it so the business doesn't fall apart during the transition?

## The Signal That You're Ready

The most reliable signal that you need an assistant is this: you are doing tasks every day that someone else could do for $20–25 an hour, and those tasks are eating time you could spend on activities that produce $200–500 an hour.

You are a high-leverage activity. Prospecting calls are a high-leverage activity. Listing appointments are a high-leverage activity. Preparing transaction documents, scheduling inspections, ordering signs, sending disclosures — these are not high-leverage activities. They're necessary. They're just not necessary for you to do personally.

:::callout
**The $200/Hour Test:** Make a list of everything you did in the last five business days. For each task, ask: could a capable person do this for $20/hour? If the answer is yes, it's a task you should eventually delegate. If 30% or more of your time is going to these tasks, you needed an assistant yesterday.
:::

## What to Hire First: Transaction Coordinator vs. Administrative Assistant

These are different roles and the right answer depends on where your bottleneck is.

| Role | Handles | Right For |
|------|---------|-----------|
| Transaction Coordinator (TC) | Everything from contract to close | Agents doing 15+ transactions/year who are drowning in paperwork |
| Administrative Assistant | Scheduling, marketing, database management, general admin | Agents who need to protect prospecting time |
| Both | Full business support | Agents doing 30+ transactions with a team |

For most agents making their first hire, a part-time transaction coordinator is the highest-leverage starting point. A good TC can handle 15–20 transactions per month without breaking a sweat. If you're doing 20+ deals a year, a TC pays for itself in the time you get back.

## The Pre-Hire Checklist

Before you hire anyone, you need documented systems. This is the step most agents skip, and it's why their first hire fails.

:::phase
### Step 1: Document Your Transaction Process
Write down every step of your transaction process, from accepted offer to close. Not a checklist — a narrative. "After we receive the accepted offer, I email it to the lender and my client within 2 hours. I then..." If you can't write it down, you can't delegate it.

### Step 2: Build Your Templates
Every email you send repeatedly should be a template. Every document you fill out regularly should have a standard approach. Create these before you hire. Your assistant will use them from day one.

### Step 3: Define the Role Precisely
Write a one-page job description. What does this person do? What do they never touch? What decisions can they make independently? What requires your approval? Vague roles produce vague results.

### Step 4: Set Success Metrics
How will you know in 90 days if this hire is working? Define 3–5 specific metrics before you hire. Transactions closed without your direct involvement. Response time to clients. Error rate on documents. Something measurable.
:::

## The Compensation Structure

For a part-time TC, the most common models:

- **Per-transaction fee:** $350–600 per closed transaction, paid at closing. Low risk for you — you only pay when the deal closes.
- **Monthly retainer:** $1,500–2,500/month for a set number of transactions. Predictable for both parties.
- **Hourly:** $20–30/hour. Works for administrative assistants. Complicated for TCs due to variable workload.

Most agents start with the per-transaction fee model because it aligns incentives and eliminates fixed cost risk.

## The First 30 Days

Your first hire will be rocky. This is normal. Budget time for training, expect mistakes, and resist the urge to take tasks back when things go wrong.

Taking tasks back teaches your assistant that you'll rescue them when things get hard, which means they never fully own the responsibility. When something goes wrong, fix it together and use it as a training moment.

## FAQ

**Can I afford to hire someone?**
Work backward from your GCI. If you're closing $150,000/year and spending 25% of your time on tasks a TC could handle, that's $37,500 of your time on $25/hour work. A TC at $400/transaction for 20 deals costs $8,000 annually. The math usually works.

**Should I use a virtual TC or hire locally?**
Both work. Virtual TCs are cheaper and easier to find. Local TCs offer more flexibility and can attend signings. For your first hire, virtual is the lower-risk starting point.

**What if I hire someone and it doesn't work out?**
Have a 90-day trial period built into your agreement. Evaluate against the success metrics you defined before hiring. If it's not working at 90 days, make a decision. Don't keep a bad hire out of guilt.
